Reading your assessment

Three numbers decide your tax bill. Most people only ever see the last one.

Assessed value

What your municipality says your property is worth for tax purposes. Not necessarily what the home would sell for. In towns that have not revalued in years, it can be far off.

The tax rate

Set every year by your municipality, county, and school district combined. Your assessment stays flat while this moves, which is why your bill climbs without anything changing about your house.

The equalization ratio

The state’s measure of how far your town’s assessments have drifted from real market values. Divide your assessment by the ratio and you get the market value your town is implying.

The number that actually matters for an appeal

Take your assessed value and divide it by your town’s equalization ratio. That is the market value your assessment implies. If your home would realistically sell for meaningfully less than that figure, you have a case worth building. New Jersey’s Chapter 123 rule gives municipalities a 15 percent cushion, so the gap has to be real, not marginal. Appeals are generally due April 1, or May 1 in a town that recently revalued.

Common questions

Where does this data come from?

The New Jersey Office of GIS publishes a statewide parcel layer joined to the Division of Taxation MOD-IV assessment records. MOD-IV is the system municipal tax assessors actually work in. Public data, refreshed roughly once a year in late spring or early summer. Aerial imagery and parcel boundaries render from Esri World Imagery.

Why is the owner name missing?

Owner names are stripped from the public version of this dataset under Daniel’s Law, which restricts disclosure of certain personal information in New Jersey. Property characteristics, assessments, and tax figures are still public.

Is the tax figure my current bill?

No. It is the prior year billed amount from the most recent published file. Rates are set annually, so your current bill will be different. Treat it as a solid ballpark. For the exact current figure, check your municipal tax collector.

Who pays the transfer fee when I sell?

The seller pays the general realty transfer fee at closing, on a graduated schedule tied to sale price. A change that caught a lot of people off guard: for contracts executed on or after July 10, 2025, residential sales over $1,000,000 carry a graduated percent fee that is now the seller’s responsibility instead of the buyer’s, and it applies to the entire sale price, not just the amount above a million. Sellers 62 and over, or who are blind or disabled, may qualify for a partial exemption on Form RTF-1. The proceeds calculator above accounts for all of it.

My address did not come up. Why?

Usually one of four things. Newly built homes may not be in the parcel file yet. Condominium units sometimes sit under a parent parcel. Properties billed as multiple lots together appear only once. Or the address needs the town and zip added. Try the full address first, then try without the unit number.

Am I paying too much?

Compare the effective rate in your results against neighbors on the same street. If yours is noticeably higher for a similar home, either your assessment is out of line or you are missing a deduction you qualify for. Both are fixable. That is the conversation worth having.

Do I qualify for ANCHOR or Stay NJ?

Very possibly. ANCHOR pays homeowners up to $1,500 and renters up to $700. Stay NJ pays seniors 65 and over up to $6,500. Most people who qualify never claim it. Run the free estimator and find out in about 90 seconds.
