The equalization ratio

Almost no New Jersey town assesses at full market value. The state publishes a ratio for every municipality each year. Divide the assessment by it and you get the value your town is implying.

What it cannot see

Public records stop at the front door. Condition, upgrades, and interior finish are exactly what an assessor weighs, and none of it is in the data. That is why this is a screening tool, not an appraisal.

The number that decides an appeal

New Jersey's Chapter 123 rule says your assessment stands if it sits at or below true market value times your town's equalization ratio times 1.15. That 15 percent is the cushion municipalities get. Above it, the county board is directed to reduce your assessment. Appeals are generally due April 1, or May 1 in a town that recently revalued. I prepare these, and I will tell you plainly when a case is not worth filing.

Common questions

Where does this data come from?

The New Jersey Office of GIS publishes a statewide parcel layer joined to the Division of Taxation MOD-IV assessment records. MOD-IV is the system municipal tax assessors actually work in. Public data, refreshed roughly once a year in late spring or early summer. Aerial imagery and parcel boundaries render from Esri World Imagery.

Does this tell me if a home is for sale?

No, and I would rather be straight with you about it. Live listing status lives in the MLS, which is a licensed feed and not public data. This page reads public assessment records only. The one thing it can tell you is when a deed was recorded recently, meaning the property changed hands, and that comes straight from the state file. For current listing status on a specific property, just ask me. I have MLS access and it takes about 30 seconds.

Why is the owner name missing?

Owner names are stripped from the public version of this dataset under Daniel’s Law, which restricts disclosure of certain personal information in New Jersey. Property characteristics, assessments, and tax figures are still public.

Is the tax figure my current bill?

No. It is the prior year billed amount from the most recent published file. Rates are set annually, so your current bill will be different. Treat it as a solid ballpark. For the exact current figure, check your municipal tax collector.

Who pays the transfer fee when I sell?

The seller pays the general realty transfer fee at closing, on a graduated schedule tied to sale price. A change that caught a lot of people off guard: for contracts executed on or after July 10, 2025, residential sales over $1,000,000 carry a graduated percent fee that is now the seller’s responsibility instead of the buyer’s, and it applies to the entire sale price, not just the amount above a million. Sellers 62 and over, or who are blind or disabled, may qualify for a partial exemption on Form RTF-1. The net proceeds calculator accounts for all of it.

My address did not come up. Why?

Usually one of four things. Newly built homes may not be in the parcel file yet. Condominium units sometimes sit under a parent parcel. Properties billed as multiple lots together appear only once. Or the address needs the town and zip added. Try the full address first, then try without the unit number.

Am I paying too much?

Compare the effective rate in your results against neighbors on the same street. If yours is noticeably higher for a similar home, either your assessment is out of line or you are missing a deduction you qualify for. Both are fixable. That is the conversation worth having.

Do I qualify for ANCHOR or Stay NJ?

Very possibly. ANCHOR pays homeowners up to $1,500 and renters up to $700. Stay NJ pays seniors 65 and over up to $6,500. Most people who qualify never claim it. Run the free estimator and find out in about 90 seconds.
