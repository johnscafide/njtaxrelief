## EM-N03: Mon Oct 19, Phase 1

Subject: Chapter 123 in four sentences
Preview: The corridor, the ratio, and what "testable" means on a desk card.

Body:

Good morning.

**Explain this to a client this week: Chapter 123.**

Every New Jersey town gets an average ratio each year: assessed value divided by market value, measured from recent verified sales. Around that ratio sits a corridor of 15% on either side, the common level range. If a home's own ratio (its assessment divided by its actual market value) sits above the top of that corridor, there is a case for review. If it sits inside the corridor, there is generally no relief, even if the number feels wrong.

That is why "my taxes are too high" is not an appeal argument and "my ratio is above the range" is. When a Watchdog desk card says "testable Chapter 123 position," it means the assessment sits above the corridor based on sourced evidence. It does not mean an appeal wins. Say "worth a review before the deadline," not "you will win."

Subject: The added-assessment bill your client got
Preview: What it is, why it showed up in October, and the December 1 deadline.

Body:

Good morning.

**Explain this to a client this week: added assessments.**

When a homeowner finishes work that adds value (an addition, a finished basement, a major renovation with a permit), the assessor adds the value to the assessment, prorated from the month it was completed. The bill for that added value mails in October, on top of the regular quarters. It surprises people every year, especially past clients who bought a fixer and finished it.

The deadline to appeal an added or omitted assessment is December 1, or 30 days from the bulk mailing of the added-assessment bills, whichever is later. That is three weeks from today. ## EM-N07: Mon Nov 16, Phase 2

Subject: Revaluation vs reassessment, plainly
Preview: The two words clients mix up, and the postcard that goes out the week notices mail.

Body:

Good morning.

**Explain this to a client this week: revaluation vs reassessment.**

A revaluation is a full redo of every assessment in town, usually by an outside firm, ordered when the town's ratio has drifted too far from market. A reassessment is a similar reset done by the assessor's office, and in some counties (Monmouth and Gloucester, for example) it happens on a rolling annual basis. Both reset assessments toward 100% of market value.

The part clients get wrong: a higher assessment after a revaluation does not automatically mean a higher bill. The tax rate is recalculated against the new total base. Some bills go up, some go down, most move a little. The honest message: "Your assessment will look very different. Whether your bill moves depends on how your house changed relative to the town. Let's look when the notice comes." Town Compare shows revaluation status for any of the 564 municipalities.

## EM-N08: Mon Nov 23, Phase 2, Thanksgiving week

Subject: Bill went up, assessment didn't. Why
Preview: Rate versus assessment, your verified share page, and a short week.

Body:

Good morning. Short week, short issue.

**Explain this to a client this week: the rate moved, not the assessment.**

A tax bill is the assessment times the tax rate. The rate is the town, county, and school budgets divided by the total assessed base. So a bill can go up while the assessment stays exactly the same, because the budget grew or the base shrank. Clients assume the assessor did something. Usually the assessor did nothing and the budget did.

The message: "Your assessment has not changed since [year]. The rate went from [X] to [Y]. That is a budget question, not an assessment question." Watchdog shows both lines with the source, so you are not guessing which one moved. Careful with the follow-up: a rate increase is not grounds for an appeal. Only the assessment is.

Subject: The February bill is an estimate
Preview: Preliminary quarters explained, clean your sphere before January, quiet week ahead.

Body:

Good morning.

**Explain this to a client this week: the first two quarters are estimates.**

The February 1 and May 1 tax bills in New Jersey are preliminary. They are based on half of the prior year's total tax, split in two, because the new budget and rate are not certified yet. The August 1 and November 1 bills carry the certified rate and true up the year. So when a client says "my taxes went down in February," the honest answer is "that is an estimate; the real number shows up in the summer bill." Same when it goes up.

This one prevents a lot of bad math on listing sheets. If a seller's "annual tax" is two preliminary quarters doubled, it is wrong.
