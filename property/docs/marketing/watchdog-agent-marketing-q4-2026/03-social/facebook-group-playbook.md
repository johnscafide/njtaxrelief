# Facebook group playbook: NJ real estate agent groups, Q4 2026

Read `00-brief/product-and-brand-brief.md` first. This playbook is how John (and, where allowed, the Coordinator) shows up in the Facebook groups where New Jersey agents actually spend time, without getting removed and without being ignored.

The whole thing rests on one idea: agents in groups have a low tolerance for vendors and a high tolerance for the person who answers the tax question nobody else in the thread can. Be the second person. Every post below is useful even if Watchdog did not exist.

### Thresholds

- Size: 500 members or more for county groups; 2,000 or more for statewide groups. Below that, engagement is usually one admin and their friends.
- Activity: at least 5 member posts in the last 7 days and comments on most of them. A 20,000-member group with three posts a month is dead.
- Ratio: if more than half of recent posts are vendor pitches, the group is a billboard, not a community. Skip it or use it only on its promo day.
- Admin presence: look for an admin who comments and removes spam. That is the group worth respecting the rules in.

### Read the rules first, every time

Before joining, read the group description and the pinned rules. Write down three things in the target list: whether promotion is allowed at all, which day (many allow "Vendor Friday" or "Self-promo Sunday"), and whether links are allowed in posts or only in comments. If the rules say "no vendors," you may still join and answer questions honestly, but you never mention Watchdog unless someone asks you directly.

When the rules are unclear, message the admin (script in Part 8). Do not guess.

1. **Statewide NJ agent groups.** Largest reach, most noise. Post the value posts on your allowed days. Comment daily. Mention Watchdog only when asked or on promo day.

7. **Luxury groups.** Assessment-to-market gaps are widest here and the sellers are the most confused by them. Teach the ratio conversation. Never name a town as "unfair."

## Part 2: The 90/10 rule

Nine value comments or posts for every one mention of Watchdog. Count them. Keep a tally in the target sheet.

Watchdog is mentioned only in two situations:
1. Someone asks. "What are you using for that?" "Is there a tool?" "Where do you get the ratio?" Then answer in one or two sentences and say you built it.
2. The group's promo day, in the format the rules allow, once per promo day, never two weeks in a row in the same group.

Everything else is a value post or a helpful comment with no product mention and no link in the body. If a value post gets a "what tool" comment, answer in the comments with the "if asked" reply for that post.

Three more rules that keep you in the group:
- Never DM a member a pitch. If someone DMs you first, answer the question, then answer about Watchdog only if they ask.
- Never use the seller-intent vocabulary the brief bans in section 6 (the words that treat an owner as a prospect because of a public record). Watchdog reads property records. Property data is not seller intent. If someone in the thread frames it that way, correct it gently and move on.
- Never tag people into a thread to boost it. Never ask for likes.

### FBG-002
- Week of: Mon Sep 28, 2026 (post Thu Oct 1)
- Phase: 0
- Topic: Assessment vs market value
- Post:

Today is the valuation date, so a good day for this one. The assessment on a New Jersey tax bill is a tax-administration value. It is not the town's opinion of the sale price.

In most towns, assessed values sit well below market on purpose. Statewide, the median assessment-to-market ratio is 55.2 percent. So a $400,000 assessment in a town at 55 percent implies something closer to $727,000 in market terms. Not an appraisal, just the ratio math.

The sentence I give agents for sellers: "Your assessment is a tax number, not a price. The state publishes a ratio for our town every year, and that ratio is how you translate it." Then you go to the sales, because sales set the price.

### FBG-003
- Week of: Mon Oct 5, 2026 (post Tue Oct 6)
- Phase: 0
- Topic: Equalization ratios
- Post:

Why is one town assessed near 95 percent of value and the town next door near 40? Because New Jersey towns revalue on their own schedules. A town that revalued last year sits near 100 percent. A town that has not touched assessments in fifteen years drifts down as prices climb.

The Division of Taxation calculates a ratio for each of the 564 municipalities every year (assessed value divided by true value, based on usable sales). Counties use it to equalize, so a town with low assessments does not pay a smaller share of county taxes.

For agents the ratio is a translator. Assessment divided by ratio gives the implied market value. A "low" assessment in a low-ratio town is not a bargain. It is just a low ratio.

- Post:

"How do I find the assessment on a property?" comes up every week, so here are the public places it lives.

### FBG-005
- Week of: Mon Oct 12, 2026 (post Tue Oct 13)
- Phase: 1
- Topic: Chapter 123 in plain words
- Post:

Chapter 123 in plain words, because agents get asked and most explanations are written for lawyers.

Each year the state certifies a ratio for every town: assessed value divided by true value. Chapter 123 draws a corridor 15 percent either side of that ratio, called the common level range. Take the property's own ratio (assessment divided by what it is actually worth). If it lands above the top of the corridor, the property has a testable overassessment position. If it lands inside, the assessment stands even when it feels high.

Two cautions. In a revaluation or reassessment year the corridor does not apply; those years use a different standard. And a screen is not an appeal. The decision belongs to the owner and their tax attorney, and the county board is the venue.

### FBG-007
- Week of: Mon Oct 19, 2026 (post Tue Oct 20)
- Phase: 1
- Topic: Revaluations
- Post:

A revaluation is not a tax increase. Tell sellers that first, because the letter from the town scares them.

A revaluation resets every assessment to current market value so the burden is shared by today's values instead of values from years ago. The total assessed base goes up, so the tax rate comes down. Some owners pay more afterward, some pay less, most land close to where they were. The ones who pay more are the ones whose values rose faster than the town average.

What agents should do: know whether a town is in a revaluation or reassessment year before quoting "the taxes" to a buyer, because the assessment and the rate are both about to move. And expect the appeal deadline to shift to May 1 in that town.

### FBG-011
- Week of: Mon Nov 2, 2026 (post Tue Nov 3)
- Phase: 2
- Topic: Added assessments
- Post:

Added assessments are the tax surprise most buyers never see coming.

When a property is improved after October 1 (an addition, a finished basement, new construction completing), the assessor adds value for the part of the year the improvement existed. That bill arrives as a separate line, usually in October, and it is prorated. The next year it is folded into the regular assessment.

Two things for us. A buyer of new construction or a fresh renovation should expect an added assessment, and their first full-year bill will be higher than whatever was on the listing. Say that in writing before they close. And added or omitted assessments have their own appeal deadline: December 1, or 30 days from the bulk mailing of the bills, whichever is later.

### FBG-016
- Week of: Mon Nov 16, 2026 (post Thu Nov 19)
- Phase: 2
- Topic: Assessment went up, bill did not (rate math)
- Post:

"My assessment went up 40 percent and my taxes barely changed. How?" This one confuses everyone, including agents.

Assessment times rate equals bill. When a town revalues, every assessment goes up to current value, so the total assessed base goes up. The town still needs the same budget, so the rate drops to match. If your house rose by the town average, your bill stays about where it was. If it rose faster than average, you pay more. Slower, you pay less.

The reverse happens too. Assessment flat, rate up, bill up, because the school or county budget grew. That is the year clients call and say "they raised my assessment" when nobody did.

Two numbers, every time. Assessment and rate. Which one moved?

Has a client ever blamed you for a tax increase that was really the rate?

A quieter concept that matters more than people think: assessment uniformity.

Two similar houses on the same street should carry similar assessments. In a town that revalued recently, they usually do. In a town that has not revalued in a long time, they drift apart, because one sold and got looked at and the other did not. The state measures this spread for every town (it is called the coefficient of deviation in the tables).

Why a buyer cares: in a low-uniformity town, the bill on the house they are buying depends partly on luck. In a high-uniformity town, it depends on the house. Neither is "unfair." It is a measurement, and it tells you how much to trust the listing's tax number.

### CT-09: "What is Chapter 123 and does my client have an appeal?"

A: The state certifies a ratio for every town each year. Chapter 123 draws a corridor 15 percent either side of it (the common level range). If the property's own ratio, assessment divided by market value, sits above the top of the corridor, there is a testable position. Inside the corridor, the assessment stands. Whether to appeal is the owner's call with a tax attorney and the county board, not ours. Revaluation years use a different standard.

If the client thinks the added value is high, the county board takes appeals until December 1 or 30 days from the bulk mailing of those bills, whichever is later.
