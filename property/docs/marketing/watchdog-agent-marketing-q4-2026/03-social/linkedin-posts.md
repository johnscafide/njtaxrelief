## LI-001 | 2026-09-28 (Mon) | Phase 0 | Type: story | Track: teaching

**Post**

A seller tells you her assessment proves the house is worth $612,000. It does not.

In New Jersey the assessment is a tax-administration number. It exists so the town can split the levy across every property. It is not an appraisal and it is not a list price.

Here is the piece most people skip. Every town has an equalization ratio published by the Division of Taxation. It is the average relationship between assessments and actual sale prices in that town. Statewide, the median assessment-to-market ratio sits at 55.2%.

So a $612,000 assessment in a town at a 55.2% ratio implies a value near $1.1 million on paper. In a town that just revalued to 100%, the same assessment implies $612,000. Same number, two completely different stories.

Before you answer the seller, look up the town's ratio. Then say: "Your assessment is a tax number. The market decides the price. Here is what the town's own ratio says about it."

Two houses, both assessed at $400,000, in two different towns. One owner is assessed at market. The other is assessed at half. Here is how you tell.

The equalization ratio. Every New Jersey municipality gets one from the Division of Taxation each year. It is the average ratio of assessed value to sale price across the town's usable sales.

## LI-007 | 2026-10-06 (Tue) | Phase 0 | Type: contrarian | Track: teaching

**Post**

The tax figure on the listing sheet is last year's bill. Sometimes it is two years old. Buyers treat it as a quote.

Here is how to read a New Jersey tax bill so you can explain it in one minute.

The bill has four quarters: February 1, May 1, August 1, November 1. The first two are preliminary, based on the prior year. The town sets the actual rate in the summer, and the third and fourth quarters catch up. That is why August feels like a surprise.

The bill also shows the assessed value, the tax rate per $100, and usually a breakdown across municipal, school, and county. Rate times assessment, divided by 100, is the annual bill.

What it does not show: the town ratio, whether a revaluation is coming, or whether an added-assessment bill is on the way for the addition the seller just finished.

So when a buyer asks "is this what I will pay," the honest answer is "this is what the last owner paid, and here is what could change it."

## LI-012 | 2026-10-13 (Tue) | Phase 1 | Type: teaching | Track: teaching

**Post**

Chapter 123 is the rule that decides most New Jersey tax appeals, and almost nobody outside the county board can explain it.

Here is the short version.

Every town has an average ratio of assessment to market value. The law draws a corridor around it: 15% above and 15% below. That corridor is the common level range.

Take a town with a 60% ratio. The corridor runs from 51% to 69%.

Now take a house assessed at $450,000 that the evidence says is worth $600,000. Its own ratio is 75%. That is above the top of the corridor. The board would revise the assessment to $600,000 times 60%, which is $360,000.

Same house, but the evidence says it is worth $700,000. Its ratio is 64%. Inside the corridor. No change, even though it is assessed above the town average.

That is why "my assessment is higher than the neighbor's" wins nothing on its own. The question is where the property sits against the corridor.

## LI-016 | 2026-10-19 (Mon) | Phase 1 | Type: teaching | Track: teaching

**Post**

A revaluation does not raise the town's taxes. It moves the same bill between neighbors. Sellers need that sentence.

When a New Jersey town revalues, every property gets a new assessment at 100% of market value as of October 1. The town then resets the tax rate so the total levy stays the same.

The levy is the pie. The revaluation changes the size of each slice.

If your house was under-assessed compared to the rest of town, your slice grows. If it was over-assessed, your slice shrinks. If it was about average, your bill barely moves even though the assessment may have doubled.

What to tell a seller in a revaluation town:
- The new assessment is not a list price.
- The bill effect is not known until the new rate is struck.
- Buyers will ask. Keep the letter from the revaluation firm handy.
- The appeal deadline after a completed revaluation is May 1, not April 1. Confirm with the county board.

What to tell a buyer: budget on the equalized value, not the old assessment.

## LI-022 | 2026-10-27 (Tue) | Phase 1 | Type: teaching | Track: teaching

**Post**

An added-assessment bill shows up in October, is due November 1, and can be appealed until December 1. Most owners have never heard of it.

Here is how it works in New Jersey.

The assessor values property as of October 1. If an improvement is completed after that date, a new house, an addition, a finished basement with a permit, the assessor adds value for the part of the year after completion. That is the added assessment. The bill arrives separately in October, on top of the regular bill, and covers the prorated months.

The appeal deadline is December 1, or 30 days from the bulk mailing of the added-assessment bills, whichever is later. County Board of Taxation.

Why agents should care:
- New construction buyers. The taxes on the listing sheet reflect the land. The added assessment is the house.
- Sellers who renovated. The permit closed. The bill is coming. Better they hear it from you.
- Buyers under contract on a renovated house. Ask whether an added assessment has been issued or is pending.

## LI-061 | 2026-12-23 (Wed) | Phase 3 | Type: teaching | Track: teaching

**Post**

Three numbers to know about any New Jersey house before you show it. Takes one minute. Saves the whole conversation.

1. The assessment. Not as a value, as a tax number. It is what the town uses to split the levy.

2. The town's ratio. Divide the assessment by it and you get the implied value the tax system is using. The statewide median is 55.2%. Your town may be anywhere from there to 100%.
