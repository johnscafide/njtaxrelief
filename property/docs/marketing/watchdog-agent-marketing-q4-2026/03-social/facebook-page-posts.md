Thursday is October 1. In New Jersey that is the assessment valuation date. Whatever a property is worth on October 1, 2026 is what assessors are supposed to be measuring for the 2027 tax year. Notices come out around February 1. Regular appeal deadlines land April 1, or May 1 in towns that finished a revaluation or reassessment, or January 15 in Monmouth, Gloucester, and Burlington under the alternate calendar. Why agents should care: a sale you close in November prices the house as of November. The assessor is looking at October 1. When a buyer asks why the assessment "does not match what we paid," the date is half the answer. The ratio is the other half. More on that Friday.

- Date: Fri Oct 2, 2026

The simplest thing you can teach a client about a New Jersey tax bill: assessment times the tax rate equals the bill. Two numbers.

The assessment is set by the municipal assessor. The rate is set after the county, school, and municipal budgets are finalized, usually in summer. So a bill can go up while the assessment sits still, because the rate moved. And an assessment can jump while the bill barely changes, because the rate came down after a revaluation.

When a client says "my assessment went up 30 percent," the next question is always "and what happened to the rate?" Look the property up, read the tax history, and you have the answer before the client finishes the sentence.

The conversation every NJ listing agent has at least once a month. Seller: "The town says my house is worth $420,000, so why are you telling me to list at $700,000?"

Here is the plain answer. The town is not saying the house is worth $420,000. The town is saying the assessed value is $420,000, and in most towns assessed values sit well below market on purpose. ### FB-005
- Date: Wed Oct 7, 2026
- Phase: 0
- Post:

Chapter 123 sounds like a law school topic. For agents it is one idea. Every year the state certifies a ratio for each town: assessed value divided by true market value. Chapter 123 draws a corridor around that ratio, 15 percent either side, called the common level range. If a property's own ratio (its assessment divided by its market value) sits above the top of that corridor, it has a testable overassessment position. Inside the corridor, the assessment stands even if it looks high.

Two cautions. Revaluation and reassessment years follow a different standard, so the corridor does not apply. And a screen is not an appeal. Watchdog shows the Chapter 123 position with the certified ratio. The decision belongs to the owner and their tax counsel.

### FB-008

"Why is Town A assessed near 90 percent of value and Town B near 40?" Because New Jersey towns revalue on their own schedules. A town that revalued last year sits near 100 percent. A town that has not revalued in fifteen years drifts down as prices rise. The Division of Taxation calculates a ratio for each of the 564 municipalities every year so county taxes can be shared fairly.

For an agent, the ratio is the translator. Assessment divided by the ratio gives the town's implied market value for that property. It is not an appraisal, but it tells a buyer whether a "low" assessment is a bargain or just a low ratio. Town Compare puts two towns' ratios and rates side by side with the year attached.

### FB-011
- Date: Wed Oct 21, 2026
- Phase: 1
- Post:

A town revaluation is not a tax increase. Say that to sellers first. A revaluation resets every assessment to current market value so the burden is shared by today's values instead of values from years ago. The tax rate then drops, because the total assessed base went up.

Some owners pay more after a revaluation, some pay less, most land near where they were. The ones who pay more are the ones whose values rose faster than the town average.

What agents should do: check whether a town is in a revaluation or reassessment year before quoting "the taxes," because the assessment and the rate are both about to change. Watchdog flags revaluation status in the municipal context of every property view.

### FB-017
- Date: Wed Nov 4, 2026
- Phase: 2
- Post:

Added assessments are the tax surprise most buyers never see coming. When a property is improved after October 1 (an addition, a finished basement, new construction completing), the assessor adds value for the part of the year the improvement existed. That bill arrives as a separate line, usually in October, and it is prorated.

Two agent facts. A buyer of new construction or a recently renovated house should expect an added assessment, and the first full-year bill will be higher than the one on the listing sheet. And added or omitted assessments have their own appeal deadline: December 1, or 30 days from the bulk mailing of the added-assessment bills, whichever is later. Confirm with the county board.

The Watchdog Score, powered by the ROBUST Framework, is a 0 to 100 read on a property's tax position. ROBUST is six things: Recourse (is there a path to review), Overassessment Position (Chapter 123 context), Burden (tax load relative to peers), Uniformity (how evenly the town assesses), Stability (how much the numbers move year to year), Trajectory (where they are heading).

The evidence sits beside the score. Every input shows its source and date. A high score is not an appeal recommendation and a low score is not a warning. It is a summary of the record so you can read it fast and then look at the parts that matter for the conversation in front of you.

### FB-035
- Date: Fri Dec 18, 2026
- Phase: 3
- Post:

Winter buyers ask the carrying-cost question more carefully. Here is the honest way to answer "what will the taxes be?"

Start with the current annual bill from the tax history, not the listing sheet. Check whether the town is in or entering a revaluation or reassessment, because both the assessment and the rate will change. Check for permits closed in the last year, which can mean an added assessment. Then say: "Here is this year's bill, here is what is scheduled to change, and here is the source."

You are not predicting the bill. You are showing the record and the moving parts. That is a better answer than a number with nothing behind it.
