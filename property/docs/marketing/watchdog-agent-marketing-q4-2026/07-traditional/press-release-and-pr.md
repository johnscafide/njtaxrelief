## PR-OPED-01. Op-ed outline (target 700 words)

Working title: Why every New Jersey listing conversation should start with the tax bill

Target outlet: [NJ.com opinion, NJ Spotlight News, NJBIZ, or ROI-NJ; verify submission rules and word limit]. Submit Tuesday October 20. Byline: John Scafide, founder of Watchdog. Disclosure line at the end: "John Scafide is the founder of Watchdog, a New Jersey property intelligence platform. Watchdog is not an MLS, an appraisal, or a legal opinion."

Section plan with word budgets:

1. Open with the driveway (90 words). A buyer standing in a driveway asks why this house pays more than the one across the street. The agent says "the town is high." The buyer nods. Both of them are guessing. Do that scene, no product.

2. The bill is the story (110 words). In New Jersey the tax bill is the second-largest housing cost after the mortgage and often the first thing a buyer's lender looks at. It is set by three things the listing never shows: the assessment, the town's assessment-to-market ratio, and the tax rate. State the statewide median ratio, 55.2%, and say what it means in one sentence: the assessment is a tax-administration number, not a value.

3. What the calendar does to a deal (120 words). Oct 1 valuation date. Notices about Feb 1. April 1 appeal deadline, May 1 in revalued towns, Jan 15 in Monmouth, Gloucester, and Burlington. Dec 1 for added assessments after a renovation. A deal that closes in March in a town mid-revaluation has a different tax future than one that closes in August. Agents who know the calendar can explain it. Agents who do not, cannot.

4. Chapter 123, in three sentences (80 words). The mechanism that decides whether an assessment can be reviewed, explained the way you would to a friend. No jargon beyond the name.

5. The public record is already there (100 words). Every number above is public: the county tax list, the equalization table, the SR1A sale record, the permit file. The problem is not secrecy. It is that the record is scattered across town and county sites and nobody has put it next to the listing. Say this without naming Watchdog.

6. What a good agent does with it (110 words). Look it up before the showing. Say the specific thing: "This assessment moved 12% last year and the town is not in a revaluation, so here is what that means for the bill." Tell the seller what the notice in February will look like. Tell the past client who finished a kitchen that December 1 matters. None of that is a prediction about anyone selling. It is being useful.

7. Close (90 words). The listing tells the buyer what the house looks like. The tax bill tells them what it costs to keep. Start there. One line back to the driveway. Then the disclosure line.

Notes: no competitor names; no town named as unfair; no guaranteed outcome; no seller-intent language of any kind. Use "a real reason to reach out" if the phrase is needed. Read aloud before submitting. Em dash check. Banned phrase check.
