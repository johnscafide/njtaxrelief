## The broker pitch

Why a broker says yes, in the order they care about it:

1. Their agents get 30 minutes of New Jersey property-tax education they cannot get anywhere else, in plain language, from someone who does this all day. Assessment vs market value, Chapter 123, the October 1 valuation date, added assessments, what a buyer actually pays.
2. No selling until the last five minutes. The broker can say that to the office and it is true.
3. Lunch is on us.
4. An office offer: every attendee gets a Founding Agent invite while seats last, and an office reward when five agents go paid (see "Office offer options").
5. It makes the broker look good. Their agents answer the tax question on the listing appointment better than the agent across town.

### Slide 2: Why NJ agents get tax questions wrong

On slide: Three misreads. 1. The listing tax figure is read as the buyer's future bill. 2. The assessment is treated as a market value. 3. "Your taxes are high, appeal" without knowing the town's ratio. 564 municipalities, each with its own ratio and its own revaluation schedule.

Talk track: Agents get property tax questions wrong for three reasons, and none of them is carelessness. One: the tax figure on the listing sheet gets read as the buyer's future bill. It is last year's bill on an assessment that can change. Two: assessed value gets treated like a market value. In most of New Jersey it is a fraction of market value, set for tax administration. Three: "your taxes are high, you should appeal" gets said without knowing the town's ratio, which is the only thing that decides an appeal. New Jersey has 564 municipalities, each with its own ratio and its own revaluation schedule. That is why this is hard, and why the agent who gets it right stands out.

### Slide 3: Assessment vs market value

On slide: Assessment is a tax-administration value. The town's average ratio relates it to market value. Statewide median: 55.2% of market value. Illustration: $300,000 assessment in a 50% town implies about $600,000 equalized value.

Talk track: An assessment is a tax-administration value. The town's average ratio, published by the state every year, tells you how that assessment relates to market value. The statewide median is about 55 percent. So a house assessed at $300,000 in a town with a 50 percent ratio is being treated as if it is worth about $600,000. That $600,000 is the number to compare against what you would list it for. When a client says "the town says my house is worth $300,000," the honest answer is "no, the town is saying $600,000, and here is how to check it." Watchdog shows the assessment, the ratio, and the equalized value on one screen with the source year attached.

### Slide 4: Chapter 123 in one slide

On slide: Assessment divided by market value, compared to the town's average ratio. Corridor: average ratio plus or minus 15% of itself. Outside the corridor, the assessment is adjusted to average ratio times market value. Inside, no change. Post-revaluation towns at 100%: the ceiling is 100%. The county board decides.

Talk track: Chapter 123 is the test the county board uses. Take the assessment. Divide it by the true market value, which is what you prove with comparable sales. Compare that number to the town's average ratio. There is a corridor around the average ratio, 15 percent above and below it. If the property's ratio falls outside the corridor, the board adjusts the assessment to the average ratio times market value. Inside the corridor, nothing changes, even if the taxes feel high. In a town that just revalued to 100 percent, the ceiling is 100. So "my taxes are high" is not a case. "My ratio is outside the corridor, and here are the sales" is. The Overassessment Position component of the Watchdog Score is this test, with the sales attached. The board decides, not us.

### Slide 5: The October 1 valuation date and the 2027 season

On slide: Oct 1, 2026: valuation date for 2027 assessments. About Feb 1, 2027: notices mailed. April 1: regular appeal deadline. May 1 in towns with a completed revaluation or reassessment. Jan 15 in Monmouth, Gloucester, and Burlington (alternate calendar). Confirm with the county board.

Talk track: October 1 is the valuation date. Every 2027 assessment reflects what the property was worth on October 1, 2026, which was a few weeks ago. Notices go out around February 1. The regular appeal deadline is April 1. It is May 1 in towns that completed a revaluation or reassessment, and January 15 in Monmouth, Gloucester, and Burlington under the alternate calendar. Always confirm with the county board, because towns move. What this means for you: the comparable sales that matter for a 2027 appeal are sales near October 1, 2026. You are closing those sales right now. You already hold the evidence a homeowner will need in March, and most of them have no idea.

### Slide 6: Added assessments and December 1

On slide: Work completed after Oct 1 gets an added assessment, prorated. Added-assessment bills typically mailed in October, due with the Nov 1 quarter. Appeal deadline Dec 1, or 30 days from the bulk mailing, whichever is later. Every past client who finished an addition, a basement, or a kitchen this year.

Talk track: Added assessments. When a property is improved and the work is completed after October 1, the town adds an assessment for the improvement, prorated for the months it existed. Those bills typically go out in October and are due with the November 1 quarter. The appeal deadline for an added or omitted assessment is December 1, or 30 days from the bulk mailing of the added-assessment bills, whichever is later. Think about every past client who finished an addition, a finished basement, or a kitchen this year. Some of them are opening a bill right now that they do not understand. That is a reason to call, with a date attached, and it has nothing to do with whether they are moving.

### Slide 7: Buyer carrying cost

On slide: What a buyer pays is assessment times rate, and both can move: revaluation, reassessment, added assessment for the seller's renovation, the municipal budget. The listing tax figure captures none of that. Show the history and the town context with the source. Do not promise a number.

Talk track: Buyer carrying cost. What a buyer pays is the assessment times the tax rate, and both can move after closing. A revaluation resets the assessment. A reassessment can too. The renovation the seller just finished becomes an added assessment. The municipal budget moves the rate. The tax figure on the listing sheet captures none of that. You cannot promise a buyer a number and you should not try. What you can do is show the assessment history, the current rate, the ratio, and whether the town has a revaluation in progress, with the source and date on each. That is the honest answer, and it is a better answer than the agent across the table is giving.
