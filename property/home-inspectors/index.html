What this guide is for

Use the record to prepare. Use the inspection to decide.

Public records can tell an inspector what the municipality believes exists on a parcel, when a structure was built, how the property is classified, what changed in the public record and whether the lot or surrounding area raises questions worth carrying into the inspection.

That context can make the site visit more focused, but it never replaces the physical inspection. Condition, safety, defects, systems and repair recommendations require the inspector's own observations and professional judgment.

Boundary: Watchdog is a research and preparation layer. It does not perform a home inspection, certify building condition, conduct radon testing or replace an on-site evaluation or NJDEP-certified radon professional.
01 · Workflow

Useful ways to prepare before the appointment.

Use the public record to identify questions that deserve attention when you are physically at the property.

01Confirm the parcel

Check the address, municipality, block and lot so the research follows the correct property before the inspection begins.

02Review recorded characteristics

Compare year built, property class, lot size and recorded improvements with what the client and listing materials describe.

03Look for change signals

Permits, assessment changes and transaction history can point to additions, renovations or other questions worth verifying on site.

04Prepare the handoff

When a finding needs an engineer, electrician, plumber, roofer, environmental specialist or municipal record, route it to the right professional.

05Handle radon as a separate testing workflow

Use municipal radon potential only as context. NJDEP says radon can vary widely even within the same neighborhood and recommends testing all homes; when a transaction needs a professional radon test, route it to a New Jersey DEP-certified radon business or professional.

02 · Interpretation

Public fields that can sharpen an inspection.

Each field is a clue, not a physical-condition conclusion.

Year builtUseful for preparing age-related questions about systems and materials, subject to what is actually observed.
Property classHelps confirm that the parcel is being treated as the type of property the inspector expects to visit.
Lot and parcel contextUseful for orienting boundaries, adjacent parcels and site questions before the walkaround.
Assessment changesA material change may justify asking whether improvements or additions occurred.
Permit or improvement signalsCan help the inspector identify work that may deserve documentation or closer physical review. Actual permit, inspection and certificate status should be verified with the enforcing agency.
Radon potential / testingA municipal radon-potential tier cannot determine the radon level in an individual home. NJDEP recommends testing all homes, and professional testing or mitigation in New Jersey must be performed by appropriately DEP-certified businesses or professionals.
Sale historyTransaction timing can help frame questions about renovations, occupancy changes or recent work.
03 · Hypothetical examples

How public records can improve preparation.

These examples are fictional and use no real residential address.

Recorded age does not match the listing

The public record shows an older construction year than the marketing material. The inspector notes the discrepancy and verifies what can be learned on site.

Assessment jumps after renovation

A sharp improvement-value increase appears in the record. The inspector asks what work was completed and pays closer attention to the altered area.

Large parcel, unusual site conditions

Parcel mapping shows more land and a different configuration than expected. The inspector arrives prepared to review drainage, access and exterior conditions carefully.

Recent sale and quick resale

Transaction history suggests a short ownership period. That does not prove renovation quality, but it can prompt better questions about recent work and documentation.

Low municipal radon tier, no test result

A hypothetical property is in a municipality with lower mapped radon potential, but no current transaction-specific test is available. The inspector does not infer that the home is radon-free; the client is directed to NJDEP testing guidance and, when appropriate, a certified radon professional.

04 · Before the inspection

Research checklist.

Simple questions that keep public-data preparation useful without letting it replace the inspection.

Am I looking at the correct parcel and municipality?
Do the public characteristics match what the client expects me to inspect?
Do recent changes suggest additions, renovations or system work worth asking about?
If radon is part of the transaction, am I treating mapped potential as context and using the current NJDEP testing/certification process for the actual result?
Which questions require municipal records or another specialist rather than an assumption?
Have I kept public-record discrepancies separate from physical-condition findings?
