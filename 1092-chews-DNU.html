NJ Property Tax Relief
Money Back in Your Pocket Every Year

New Jersey runs one of the most significant property tax rebate programs in the country. As a homeowner here, you will be eligible for the ANCHOR benefit starting your first full year of residency. This is real money, and most people do not realize how straightforward it is to claim.

ANCHOR Program (Affordable NJ Communities for Homeowners and Renters)

ANCHOR is a state-funded rebate paid directly to you once per year. To qualify, you must own and occupy this as your primary residence in New Jersey, and your household gross income must be $250,000 or less. That is it.

The benefit is based on your income bracket. Here is what homeowners under 65 currently receive:

$1,500 If your household income is $150,000 or less
$1,000 If your household income is $150,001 to $250,000
Bottom line: On a $10,117 annual tax bill, a $1,000 to $1,500 ANCHOR rebate brings your effective annual cost down to roughly $8,600 to $9,100 depending on your income. That is $717 to $758 per month in real property tax cost after the rebate.

How to Claim It

Applications open each fall (typically October). You apply online through the NJ Division of Taxation website using ID.me for identity verification. No PIN required anymore. Payments go out on a rolling basis starting in September of the following year -- either by check or direct deposit.

You need to have been a homeowner and resident here on October 1 of the benefit year. So if you close before October 1, 2026, you will be eligible to apply for the 2026 benefit in fall 2026.

Stay NJ is a separate senior program (50% of property taxes up to $13,000 minus ANCHOR/Senior Freeze amounts) for homeowners who are 65 or older. It does not apply here unless that describes your situation, but it is worth knowing if you are buying for a parent.
Buying With Cash
What Changes When There Is No Mortgage

Paying cash is a real competitive advantage in this market, and it also changes how several parts of the transaction work. Here is what you should know going in.

No lender-required inspections
You still want a home inspection. But without a mortgage, no lender is requiring a new termite inspection or appraisal. Those are your call -- and they protect you, so we strongly recommend keeping them in your offer.
No appraisal contingency needed
Cash buyers are not bound by a lender appraisal. This can strengthen your offer significantly. Just make sure you are comfortable with the value independently before waiving any protections.
Faster closing timeline
Without mortgage underwriting, you can close in 2 to 3 weeks if title is clear. Given that this sale is contingent on the seller's purchase, your flexibility here is actually a negotiating chip -- you can accommodate their timeline more easily.
Proof of funds required
The listing agent will expect a proof of funds letter or bank statement before accepting an offer. Have this ready before you submit -- ideally dated within 30 days.
No mortgage interest deduction
Cash buyers lose the federal mortgage interest deduction, but NJ does allow a property tax deduction on your state return (up to $15,000 for married/CU couples). ANCHOR also remains fully available to cash buyers.
Title insurance still matters
Without a lender requiring it, you may skip the lender policy -- but owner's title insurance is absolutely still worth buying. Given the recorded easements on this property, you want a clean title opinion in hand before closing.
With two recorded easements on this property, make sure your title company specifically reviews the Estates Lane ingress/egress documents and the stormwater basin agreement before you close. These are already recorded so title should be clear, but you want confirmation.
Recorded Easements and Deed Restrictions
What a Basin Is and Why It Matters Here

This subdivision (Chews Landing Estates, 5 lots total) was approved in 2008. As a condition of that approval, Gloucester Township required two permanent easements. They are recorded at the Camden County Clerk's office and bind every owner of this property forever.

What Is a Stormwater Detention Basin?

A detention basin is basically a shallow landscaped depression in the ground designed to collect rainwater runoff from the neighborhood during storms. Instead of that water rushing into streets and storm drains all at once, it collects in the basin and slowly filters into the ground over 24 to 72 hours.

Think of it like a giant sponge area. When it rains hard, it fills up temporarily. When it stops, the water drains into the soil. In between storms, it just looks like a grassy low area in someone's yard. It is not a pond. It is not a wetland. It does not hold permanent standing water when functioning correctly.

The basin here sits on the neighboring Lot 25. You cannot see it from this house, and it does not affect the property's use or enjoyment directly. What it does affect is your wallet slightly, because all five lot owners share the cost to maintain it.

Easement 1 - Estates Lane (Shared Private Driveway)

Estates Lane is a private lane that is the only vehicle access from Chews Landing-Clementon Road to this lot and two other lots behind it (Lots 26.01 and 26.02). You own the right to use it, but you do not own the lane itself outright -- it is a shared access easement.

The owner of Lot 26.01 is the designated administrator. All three lots that use the lane share maintenance costs equally. If the pavement cracks, if snow needs clearing, if anything needs repair -- that cost gets split three ways. If any owner refuses to pay, the administrator can pursue legal action.

There is no direct access to this property any other way by vehicle. Lots 25 and 27 are explicitly prohibited from using Estates Lane.

Easement 2 - Stormwater Basin (All 5 Lots Share Costs)

All five lots in the subdivision share responsibility for maintaining the basin located on Lot 25. The owner of Lot 25 administers it. Costs are split equally regardless of lot size or how much water each property contributes.

The 2008 engineering report estimated annual maintenance at $2,075.01 total -- or roughly $415 per lot per year. That covers mowing the basin 9 times per season, two inspections per year, sediment and debris removal, and erosion control work as needed. Actual costs may differ.

If the basin is neglected and the Township has to step in, they can bill the owners directly or assess it as a property tax. Non-payment can result in a lien on the property.

The seller is required to disclose this in the contract of sale and it must appear in the deed you receive at closing.

Neither easement prevents the sale or creates a problem with title. They are routine for small private subdivisions in NJ. Your attorney should review the full recorded documents during attorney review. The key thing to understand is that these are ongoing obligations -- not one-time items.
